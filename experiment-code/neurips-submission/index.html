Agent Society · Generative Performance

In a Grove, Again

One fixed moment from Akutagawa’s In a Grove, replayed — until the same instant opens into a navigable map of different realities, each authored by no one in the room, yet traceable to a single committed decision.

Generative performance · Screen-based · Real-time

The Work

Three people are frozen in a bamboo grove just after the assault: the bandit, the samurai bound to a tree, and his wife, Masago. The audience watches this same instant play forward, again and again. Each time, the three act on their own hidden agendas and private knowledge — and the grove resolves differently: a killing, a suicide, a severance, a refusal, a standoff. The people on screen, not a script, decide which.

Akutagawa gave three irreconcilable accounts of one death and refused to say which was true. His ambiguity is epistemic: a fact exists, but no one can reach it. Agent Society pushes it into the ontological. Because the event is produced rather than recalled, replaying the instant yields not rival accounts of one death but different deaths — and sometimes no death at all.

The work does not abolish the author. A human chose the text, fixed the scene and its roles, and wrote the prompts; an orchestrator shapes pressure; a model supplies the priors behind every word. It does not eliminate authorship — it distributes it across human design, character agents, orchestration, language models, and chance. What no single party holds is control over the complete plot. That missing center is the subject.

A Walkable Manifold

These histories are not a list but a space. The work has become a navigable atlas: one frozen instant at the root, every recorded run a leaf, arranged by what it became — a killing, a severance, a held standoff, an unresolved silence. Replaying the moment does not retell one death; it samples the territory of deaths, and non-deaths, the instant can reach.

Opening any resolved leaf reveals its responsibility line: the chain of already-committed decisions — a concealment, the object it bears on, the line finally spoken — that carried the instant to that end. Nothing in the line is invented after the fact; it is drawn only from what the agents logged before any prose existed, and the turns the system could not author (model fallbacks) are marked and set aside, not smoothed over.

The atlas also makes its own conditions visible. Under strong orchestration pressure the territory contracts toward a single resolution; with that pressure removed (configuration B′) it opens into meaningful unresolved states but rarely a death. And every resolved death on the map was initiated by the same person — Masago. Agency here is real and distributed, but it is not held equally.

Refitting the Past

The forward direction replays a fixed beginning into divergent futures. The work then runs the problem in reverse — Akutagawa’s actual problem. The system is handed only the aftermath as found, agentive attribution stripped: a corpse with a throat wound, a bloodied dagger, a cut rope, a missing horse. For each possible initiator it refits a complete candidate process as structured actions, replayed literally through a physical model of the grove — bindings, holdings, reachability, wounds. A physics violation triggers a targeted refit, and the precondition it forced is displayed as a derived fact: the suicide account, for instance, stands only once someone has first cut the rope — a necessity the model was compelled to write, and the same one Akutagawa himself had to.

In the case shown below, all three refitted pasts survive: she killed him; the bandit seized her blade and killed him; he drove the dagger into his own throat. Three irreconcilable accounts of one corpse, each consistent with everything a later arriver could observe. The evidence does not decide. Where Akutagawa left the contradiction to his readers, this system holds one further page: the ledger of committed decisions, written before any prose existed — and it records who moved first. The audience may open it; the magistrate never could.

The Witnesses

The reconstruction above seeks a feasible past; the testimony asks a different question, and it is the one Akutagawa really posed. Here the variable is still a reconstructed process, but the objective is each witness’s self-interest, and the only constraint is the incontestable scene: τ̂i = argmaxτ Ui(τ) subject to τ ∈ F(evidence). Each agent is told two things and no more — the facts anyone could verify, and what it wants (the bandit: to be a warrior, not an ambusher; the wife: to keep some dignity; the samurai: to die by his own hand). It is never told to lie. Given only the incentive, and drawn just once, the three reconstruct — unprompted — the exact shapes of Akutagawa’s three accounts: a glorious duel, a tragic mercy, an honourable suicide.

Because the system kept the ledger of what actually happened, each account is scored against the truth, and its distortions split cleanly in two. The bandit’s provable lie — a sword duel — the unbloodied longsword refutes on the spot. But the wife’s mercy and the samurai’s suicide are unfalsifiable bends: each is physically consistent with everything at the scene (we confirmed the suicide replays cleanly), false only against a ledger no magistrate could hold. The true killer’s account contains no provable lie at all — only a bent motive. Self-interest was not scripted; it emerged, with a mean gain of Δ = +66 over the truth. This is where authorship and agency meet the theme most sharply: the agents write not only the future, from a fixed instant, but the past, from a fixed aftermath — and what each writes is a function of what it wants.

The Optimised Witness

The witness layer measured self-interest; it did not optimise for it. So we made it optimise — the honest way to earn the word. For each witness we draw K accounts from incentive alone, discard every one a coroner could refute (enforcing τ∈F for real), and keep the feasible account that best serves the teller: τ̂i = argmaxτ Ui(τ) subject to τ ∈ F, now actually computed. Two results followed, and the honest one is the smaller.

First, the deflating one: optimisation barely did anything. The self-interest the optimiser could inject by selecting — Δopt = U(winner) − mean — came to about +2 across the three witnesses. A single draw already sits at the model’s ceiling of self-flattery (the bandit’s twelve accounts scored identically); best-of-K has almost nothing left to choose. If there is authorship in optimisation, here it is nearly nil.

The real finding is what the hard constraint exposed: self-interest is not equally compatible with the truth. The true killer serves herself entirely within the evidence — every one of Masago’s accounts is feasible (100%), because she bends only motive, never a physical fact. The samurai’s honourable suicide is feasible too (92%). But the bandit’s vanity requires a refutable lie: in twelve draws he never once produced an account that both flattered him and survived the scene (0% feasible). The truth grants him nothing — no glory, no duel — so the only story that serves him is one the unbloodied longsword destroys. Under the constraint, his problem has no solution. Whose agency the evidence can accommodate, and whose it cannot, turns out to differ by person — and that asymmetry, not the optimisation, is the result.

How Agency Is Distributed

Each turn runs in two separate stages. First, every character agent — acting only on its private knowledge and hidden goal, blind to the others’ inner states — commits a structured decision: an intent, what it conceals, what it reveals, the object it acts on. Second, a scene-writer renders that decision into prose. Subsequent agents read the structured event record, not the prose: the renderer shapes how an event is experienced, not what other characters believe occurred. Information asymmetry is structural, not narrated.

ArtistChooses text, scene, roles, rules; documents and juxtaposes runs — cannot decide any single run’s outcome.
Character agentsDecide intent, action, concealment, disclosure — cannot see another agent’s internal state.
Director agentChanges environmental affordances and the cost of waiting — may not request resolution, irreversible action, violence, or a particular dramatic shape.
Scene-writerDecides language, rhythm, tone — is instructed to add no unlogged action, object, or causal relation.
AudienceMay Replay the Same Instant — cannot intervene in a performance underway (procedural agency, not dramaturgical).
Underlying modelShapes linguistic and behavioral priors — does not independently pursue a plot-level objective.

Decision Before Prose

One real turn. The decision is logged before any prose exists; the line is rendered only from it.

Agent
Takehiro, the bound samurai
Goal
Force a duel rather than a slaughter, even at the cost of his life — to reclaim a shred of dignity.
Conceals
That his wife’s defilement is also his own unspeakable shame.
Acts on
The longsword standing in the mud.
Rendered line
“You have the wolf’s hunger, bandit, but you lack the wolf’s heart… do you want to die a husband, or a liar?”

The concealed shame never surfaces in the line, yet governs it. A single, omniscient prompt has no hidden, agent-private state to withhold; here the withholding is structural and logged. In the interactive atlas, every resolved history exposes a line like this — its outcome traced back through committed concealments to the agent who carried it there.

Development Notes

The system’s diversity turned out to depend on orchestration. Under strong resolution pressure — an orchestrator pushed to force an irreversible event — outcomes converged: the run almost always ended with the agent who held the weapon (Masago) killing the samurai, while other runs simply stalled. That pressure was, in effect, an invisible co-author: it did not name the act, but it demanded that one occur.

Removing it (configuration B′: lightweight, diegetic, non-directed environmental pressure only) swung the work to the opposite pole — early runs reached meaningful unresolved states (a bond severed by a look, a refusal at one’s own throat, an escape poisoned) but rarely a death. The richest, rarest outcomes — including the self-inflicted death in which responsibility stays divided between intention, weapon, and hand — appeared under lighter orchestration in between.

Raising stability compressed the possibility space. We chose to give up engineering success-rate in order to protect the work’s real subject: whether characters can produce an event history that no external editor pushed them toward.

The likely root cause is not pressure but asymmetry of goals and affordances: Masago holds both the most concrete, executable goal and the blade, while the men’s goals are largely verbal — honor, humiliation, hesitation. Balancing what each character can actually do, rather than tuning pressure, is the current direction.

Artistic & Technical Context

The mechanism — LLM-driven agents producing emergent narrative from social dynamics — is established: Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) for believable emergent behavior; Façade (Mateas & Stern, 2005) for autonomous dramatic characters under a drama manager; and orchestrated emergent narrative with embodied multi-agent systems (NeurIPS Creative AI, 2025). Those works ask whether emergent narrative can be made to work. Agent Society shifts the question from narrative coherence to causal authorship: a canonical instant held fixed, decisions committed before prose, and no component granted authority over the whole.

After Ryūnosuke Akutagawa, Yabu no Naka [In a Grove], 1922 (public domain).
