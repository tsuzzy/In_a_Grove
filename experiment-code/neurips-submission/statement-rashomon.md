## In a Grove, Again: Authorship Without an Author

*Agent Society* stages a single, fixed moment from Akutagawa's *In a Grove* — the bandit, the bound samurai, and his wife, frozen in the instant after the assault — and then surrenders the rest to the people in it. There is no script and no narrator. Each character is an autonomous agent acting only on its own hidden agenda and private knowledge; what it conceals never reaches the audience's ears, yet shapes every move it makes. From this one frozen instant the scene plays forward, and a death — or a flight, or a severance — emerges that no human, and no single prompt, ever wrote.

This is not a demonstration that machines can improvise coherent stories; that has been shown. It is an inquiry into where authorship goes when it is distributed across a society of self-interested agents. Akutagawa left us three irreconcilable testimonies of one death and refused to say which was true: his ambiguity is *epistemic* — a fact exists, but no one can reach it. Our agents push that ambiguity one level deeper, into the *ontological*: replay the same instant and the death itself is not singular. In one run the samurai drives the dagger into his own throat; in another his wife drives it into his; in a third he throws himself onto the blade she holds between them, so that no one — not even the audience that watched — can say whether it was suicide or murder. The truth here is not hidden behind the event. It is a function of whose agency prevailed, and it changes every time.

We treat *In a Grove* as a control. By holding the premise that a master author fixed and varying only the authorship, the work makes visible what each kind of authorship produces. The human author chose one path through the possibility space and shaped it toward meaning. The agent society, given the same starting conditions, rediscovers two of Akutagawa's three testimonies on its own and then generates endings he excluded — and we can trace each outcome back to the specific, logged decisions of agents who lied, withheld, and broke. The machinery is not hidden behind the prose; it is the work. The audience watches authorship happen with no author in the room.

We are equally interested in where this authorship fails. When the society stops cohering, no one strikes, the pressure dissipates, and the scene reverts from drama to mechanism. We do not hide these collapses. They mark the edge of machine agency — the line past which a society of agents stops adding up to a self.

*Agent Society* asks its audience to sit with an authorship that is real, consequential, and emergent, and that belongs to no one present: a story, and a death, that everyone produced and no one wrote.
